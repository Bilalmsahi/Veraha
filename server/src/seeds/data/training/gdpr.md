## SECTION 1: GDPR Overview — What It Is and Why It Matters

### The Most Significant Privacy Law in the World

The General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) came into force on May 25, 2018, replacing the EU's 1995 Data Protection Directive. It is widely regarded as the most comprehensive and influential privacy law in the world — not just in Europe, but globally, as organizations worldwide have had to adapt their practices to comply with it.

GDPR was motivated by a fundamental belief: **privacy is a human right**, not a commercial consideration. As the digital economy grew exponentially, individuals were generating unprecedented amounts of personal data — and organizations were collecting, sharing, and monetizing it with minimal transparency and accountability.

GDPR was designed to restore the balance of power between individuals and the organizations that process their data.

### Why GDPR Matters to You — Regardless of Where You Work

Here is a crucial point that many employees misunderstand: **GDPR applies based on where data subjects (individuals) are located, not where your organization is located.**

If your organization processes personal data of individuals located in the EU or EEA — even if your organization is based in the United States, India, Australia, or anywhere else — GDPR applies to that processing.

This means:
- If you handle data of EU customers, employees, or website visitors — GDPR applies
- If you run marketing campaigns targeting EU residents — GDPR applies
- If you manage EU job applicants — GDPR applies

**This is not a European company problem. It is an everyone problem.**

### The Cost of Non-Compliance

GDPR enforcement is serious and growing:

**Administrative fines under GDPR are structured in two tiers:**

| Tier | Maximum Fine | Applicable Violations |
|------|-------------|----------------------|
| Lower | €10 million or 2% of global annual revenue (whichever is higher) | Technical violations (data security, breach notification, records of processing) |
| Upper | €20 million or 4% of global annual revenue (whichever is higher) | Fundamental violations (lawful basis, data subject rights, cross-border transfers) |

**Notable enforcement examples:**
- A major tech company was fined €1.2 billion (2023) for unlawful data transfers to the US
- An airline was fined £20 million for a data breach that exposed customer data
- An e-commerce company was fined €746 million for behavioral advertising without proper consent
- A social media platform was fined €390 million for misrepresenting contractual necessity as a lawful basis for advertising

Beyond fines, GDPR violations expose organizations to reputational damage, customer loss, and individual civil claims for compensation.

### GDPR's Core Principles (Article 5)

Everything in GDPR flows from six foundational principles. Memorizing these will help you understand the "why" behind every specific requirement:

1. **Lawfulness, Fairness, and Transparency** — Processing must have a legal basis and individuals must be told how their data is used
2. **Purpose Limitation** — Data collected for one purpose cannot be used for a different, incompatible purpose
3. **Data Minimisation** — Only collect and process the minimum data necessary for the stated purpose
4. **Accuracy** — Personal data must be kept accurate and up to date
5. **Storage Limitation** — Data should not be kept longer than necessary for its purpose
6. **Integrity and Confidentiality** — Data must be processed securely to protect against unauthorized access, loss, or destruction

There is a seventh overarching principle:

7. **Accountability** — Organizations must be able to demonstrate compliance with all of the above

## SECTION 2: Personal Data — Identifying What You're Protecting

### What Is Personal Data?

Under GDPR, **personal data** is defined as any information relating to an **identified or identifiable natural person** (a "data subject").

An identifiable person is one who can be identified, directly or indirectly, by reference to an identifier such as:
- A name
- An identification number
- Location data
- An online identifier (IP address, cookie ID)
- One or more factors specific to the physical, physiological, genetic, mental, economic, cultural, or social identity of that person

### The Breadth of Personal Data

GDPR's definition of personal data is intentionally broad. Many things employees don't think of as "personal data" actually are:

| Data Item | Personal Data? | Why |
|-----------|---------------|-----|
| Customer name and email | Yes | Direct identifiers |
| IP address | Yes | Can identify a device/person |
| Cookie identifiers | Yes | Can track and identify online behavior |
| Employee number | Yes | Can identify a specific employee |
| CCTV footage | Yes | May identify individuals |
| Voice recording | Yes | Biometric identifier |
| "User ID 84729" combined with purchase history | Potentially | Combination may be identifiable |
| Truly anonymized statistical data | No | Cannot be linked back to an individual |
| A business name (no individual identified) | Generally No | Unless it identifies a sole trader |

### What Is NOT Personal Data

For data to fall outside GDPR, it must be **truly anonymous** — processed in such a way that the person cannot be identified directly or indirectly. This is a high bar.

**Pseudonymous data is NOT anonymous.** Pseudonymization (replacing names with codes or IDs) reduces risk but the data remains personal data under GDPR because it can be re-identified using the key. GDPR still applies to pseudonymized data.

**Aggregated statistical data** with no possibility of re-identification is generally not personal data. ("We had 5,000 website visitors from France last month" — not personal data. "User ID 123 from France visited 5,000 times" — personal data.)

## SECTION 3: Special Category Data — The Most Sensitive Information

### What Is Special Category Data?

GDPR recognizes that certain categories of personal data are inherently more sensitive because their misuse could create significant risks of discrimination, harm, or fundamental rights violations. These categories receive the highest level of protection and require not just a lawful basis but an **additional specific condition** for processing.

**The eight special categories are:**

1. **Racial or ethnic origin**
2. **Political opinions**
3. **Religious or philosophical beliefs**
4. **Trade union membership**
5. **Genetic data** (inherited or acquired genetic characteristics)
6. **Biometric data** processed to uniquely identify a person (fingerprints, facial recognition)
7. **Health data** (physical or mental health conditions, medical history, disability)
8. **Sex life or sexual orientation**

Additionally, **criminal conviction and offence data** receives similar heightened protection.

### Why This Matters in Practice

You might encounter special category data in unexpected contexts:

- **HR processes:** Sickness absence records (health data), equal opportunities monitoring (racial origin, disability), maternity/paternity leave records
- **Customer services:** Accessibility needs (health/disability data), dietary requirements at events (potentially reveals religious beliefs)
- **Marketing:** Profiling based on inferred political opinions or lifestyle choices
- **Security:** Biometric access control systems (fingerprint or facial recognition)

**Warning:** Processing special category data without a valid condition is one of the most serious GDPR violations. Before processing any special category data, consult your Data Protection Officer (DPO) or privacy team.

### Conditions for Processing Special Category Data

Processing is only permitted if BOTH a lawful basis (Article 6) AND a special category condition (Article 9) are met. Common conditions include:

- **Explicit consent** of the data subject
- **Employment law obligations** (e.g., statutory sick pay calculations)
- **Vital interests** (where the person cannot consent)
- **Public interest** tasks established by law
- **Health and social care purposes** by professionals bound by confidentiality obligations
- **Substantial public interest** (e.g., equality of opportunity monitoring with appropriate safeguards)

## SECTION 4: Lawful Basis for Processing

### You Must Have a Lawful Basis BEFORE You Process

This is one of the most fundamental GDPR requirements: **every time you process personal data, there must be a valid lawful basis.** You cannot process personal data simply because it would be convenient, useful, or because you have the data already.

GDPR provides six lawful bases in Article 6:

### The Six Lawful Bases

**1. Consent (Article 6(1)(a))**
The data subject has given clear, specific, informed, and unambiguous consent to the processing of their personal data for one or more specific purposes.

Requirements for valid consent:
- Freely given — cannot be a condition of service unless the processing is genuinely necessary for the service
- Specific — consent to one purpose doesn't cover other purposes
- Informed — the person must understand what they're consenting to
- Unambiguous — pre-ticked boxes do NOT constitute consent
- Easy to withdraw — you must be able to withdraw consent as easily as it was given

**When consent is NOT appropriate:** If the power imbalance between your organization and the individual is significant (e.g., employer-employee relationship), consent may not be freely given and a different basis should be used.

**2. Contract (Article 6(1)(b))**
Processing is necessary to perform a contract with the data subject, or to take pre-contractual steps at their request.

Example: Processing a customer's shipping address to deliver goods they ordered is necessary for the contract.

**Common misuse:** Organizations sometimes claim contract as a basis when the processing is actually just beneficial, not strictly necessary for the contract. If the processing could be removed without affecting contract performance, contract is not the right basis.

**3. Legal Obligation (Article 6(1)(c))**
Processing is necessary to comply with a legal obligation under EU or member state law.

Example: Processing employee payroll data to comply with tax reporting obligations.

**4. Vital Interests (Article 6(1)(d))**
Processing is necessary to protect someone's life. This is a last-resort basis and generally only applicable in emergencies.

**5. Public Task (Article 6(1)(e))**
Processing is necessary for the performance of a task carried out in the public interest or in the exercise of official authority. Primarily for public bodies.

**6. Legitimate Interests (Article 6(1)(f))**
Processing is necessary for the legitimate interests of the controller or third party, except where these are overridden by the interests or fundamental rights of the data subject.

This is the most flexible basis but also requires a **three-part balancing test:**
- Is there a legitimate interest?
- Is the processing necessary for that interest?
- Do the individual's rights and interests override the legitimate interest?

**You must document this test before relying on legitimate interests.**

Legitimate interests cannot be used by public authorities when performing their tasks.

### Choosing the Right Basis

The lawful basis must be determined **before** processing begins. You cannot collect data under one basis and then switch to another when challenged. If you're uncertain which basis applies, consult your DPO or privacy team.

> **Quick Tip:** Consent sounds intuitive but is often the wrong basis — and the hardest to maintain correctly. Consent can be withdrawn at any time, requiring you to stop processing. Where another basis applies (contract, legal obligation, legitimate interests), it is often more stable and appropriate.

## SECTION 5: Consent — Getting It Right

### The Consent Trap

Many organizations default to consent as their go-to lawful basis because it feels familiar and safe. In reality, consent under GDPR is one of the most demanding and fragile lawful bases:

- It can be withdrawn at any time, and you must stop processing immediately
- You must be able to prove consent was given (retention of records)
- Invalid consent means all processing under that basis was unlawful
- Pre-ticked boxes, bundled consent, and consent buried in Terms & Conditions are invalid

### What Valid Consent Looks Like

**Valid:** A checkbox on a registration form, unticked by default, with a clear, specific statement like: "I agree to receive marketing emails from [Company] about products and services. I can unsubscribe at any time." The checkbox must be separate from the Terms of Service agreement.

**Invalid:** A pre-ticked box saying "I agree to receive marketing from all our partner companies and third-party services."

**Invalid:** Terms of Service that include a buried clause: "By using this service, you consent to us sharing your data with our marketing partners."

**Invalid:** An employer asking employees to "consent" to health monitoring. The employment power imbalance means this consent is not freely given.

### Managing Consent Records

You must maintain records of:
- When consent was given
- What information was provided to the individual at the time
- The mechanism used to collect consent
- Any subsequent withdrawal of consent

If you cannot demonstrate valid, documented consent when challenged by a regulator or data subject, you cannot rely on it as a lawful basis.

### Consent Withdrawal

When someone withdraws consent:
- You must stop processing their data for that purpose immediately
- Withdrawal doesn't affect processing already done while consent was valid
- The process to withdraw must be as easy as giving consent in the first place

## SECTION 6: Data Minimisation and Purpose Limitation

### Collect Only What You Need

Data minimisation (Article 5(1)(c)) requires that personal data collected must be:
- **Adequate** — sufficient to properly fulfil the stated purpose
- **Relevant** — pertaining to that purpose
- **Limited** — restricted to what is necessary for that purpose

This principle should fundamentally shape how your organization collects data. Before adding a field to a form, before importing a dataset, before sending a survey — ask: **Do we genuinely need this information?**

**Common violations of data minimisation:**

- Collecting date of birth on forms where only age verification is needed (year of birth is sufficient)
- Asking for full mailing addresses when only a country/region is needed for analytics
- Collecting extensive demographic information in surveys that doesn't relate to the survey purpose
- Retaining complete raw datasets "just in case" when only aggregated summaries are needed going forward

**Scenario:** Your marketing team wants to run a newsletter campaign. They propose collecting name, email, date of birth, phone number, home address, occupation, income range, and lifestyle preferences on the subscription form. 

**Data minimisation analysis:** For a newsletter, you need an email address. A name is useful for personalization. Date of birth, phone number, home address, and income are not necessary for delivering a newsletter and should not be collected — unless there's a genuinely separate, specific purpose requiring them with its own lawful basis.

### Purpose Limitation — Data Has a Job

Purpose limitation (Article 5(1)(b)) means that personal data collected for one specific, documented purpose cannot be repurposed for something different, incompatible, or undisclosed.

**Examples:**

| Collected For | Can Be Used For | Cannot Be Used For |
|--------------|----------------|-------------------|
| Processing a customer order | Delivery, billing, warranty service | Unsolicited marketing (different purpose) |
| Employee payroll | Tax reporting, statutory compliance | Covert monitoring or profiling |
| User account management | Account security, service provision | Selling to data brokers |
| Website analytics | Understanding traffic patterns | Individual behavioral profiling for advertising (unless consented) |

**Testing compatibility:** GDPR allows personal data to be used for compatible further purposes. Factors to consider: the link between original and new purpose, the nature of the data (especially if special category), the consequences for individuals, and the existence of safeguards. Statistical or archival research in the public interest is generally compatible.

## SECTION 7: Data Retention — How Long Is Too Long?

### The Storage Limitation Principle

GDPR's storage limitation principle (Article 5(1)(e)) requires that personal data must be kept "no longer than is necessary for the purposes for which the personal data are processed."

This principle is one of the most widely violated in practice — organizations frequently retain personal data long after the original purpose has been fulfilled, simply because deletion takes effort or data is seen as potentially "useful someday."

**Someday is not a lawful basis for retention.**

### Building a Retention Schedule

Your organization should have a documented data retention schedule that specifies:

- What categories of personal data are held
- The purpose for holding each category
- The retention period and the basis for that period
- Who is responsible for deletion/anonymization when the retention period expires

**Common retention periods (examples — your organization's schedule may differ):**

| Data Category | Typical Retention Period | Basis |
|--------------|------------------------|-------|
| Employee records | Duration of employment + 6-7 years | Legal obligations (tax, employment law) |
| Customer purchase records | 7 years | Legal (tax obligations) |
| Marketing contacts (consented) | Until consent withdrawn or last meaningful contact + reasonable period | Consent basis |
| Job applicant records (unsuccessful) | 6 months to 1 year | Legitimate interests (defending claims) |
| CCTV footage | 30 days (unless related to incident) | Legitimate interests |
| Website analytics logs | 13 months | Industry standard / legitimate interests |

### Your Responsibilities

- Do not retain personal data beyond the retention period in your organization's schedule
- Do not copy personal data to personal drives, USB sticks, or shadow IT systems where it might persist beyond the official retention period
- When you are done with a personal data file or spreadsheet (used for a specific project), delete or return it rather than keeping it "just in case"
- Participate in data cleanup activities organized by the DPO or IT team

## SECTION 8: Data Subject Rights — What Individuals Can Ask For

### Individuals Have Powerful Rights

GDPR gives individuals (data subjects) eight rights over their personal data. Your organization must be able to facilitate these rights. As an employee, you need to understand them so you can recognize when a request is being made and route it correctly.

### The Eight GDPR Rights

**1. Right of Access (Article 15) — Subject Access Request (SAR)**
Individuals can ask what personal data you hold about them, why, how long you'll keep it, who it's shared with, and receive a copy. You must respond within **one month** (extendable by two additional months for complex requests). The response is generally free.

**2. Right to Rectification (Article 16)**
Individuals can ask you to correct inaccurate personal data or complete incomplete data. You have **one month** to respond.

**3. Right to Erasure / Right to be Forgotten (Article 17)**
Individuals can ask you to delete their personal data in certain circumstances: the data is no longer needed for its purpose, consent is withdrawn and there's no other lawful basis, the individual objects and you have no overriding grounds, or the data was unlawfully processed.

**Important:** This is not an absolute right. You can refuse if you need the data to comply with a legal obligation, for establishment or defence of legal claims, or for other specific reasons.

**4. Right to Restriction of Processing (Article 18)**
Individuals can ask you to restrict (pause) processing in certain circumstances — for example, while accuracy is contested or while an objection is being considered.

**5. Right to Data Portability (Article 20)**
Where processing is based on consent or contract and is carried out by automated means, individuals can receive their data in a structured, commonly used, machine-readable format, and transmit it to another controller.

**6. Right to Object (Article 21)**
Individuals can object to processing based on legitimate interests or public task (you must stop unless you can demonstrate compelling legitimate grounds that override their interests), and they have an absolute right to object to direct marketing.

**7. Rights in Relation to Automated Decision-Making (Article 22)**
Individuals can object to decisions made solely by automated means (including profiling) that produce legal or significant effects. They have the right to human review.

**8. Right to Withdraw Consent**
Where processing is based on consent, individuals can withdraw at any time and you must stop processing.

### Handling Data Subject Requests

If you receive a request from an individual about their personal data:

1. **Do not ignore it** — you have one month to respond
2. **Do not respond directly** without routing through your privacy team — responses must be coordinated
3. **Route immediately** to your DPO / Privacy Officer / Data Protection team
4. **Preserve relevant records** — do not delete any data that may be relevant to the request
5. **Document the receipt** — when, from whom, and what was requested

**Scenario:** A former customer emails your customer service inbox saying: "I want you to delete all my data — I no longer use your service and did not consent to you keeping my information." This is a Right to Erasure request. It must be routed to the privacy team immediately. Do not simply reply "OK, done" — the request requires a formal process, documentation, and a proper response letter.

## SECTION 9: Privacy by Design and Default

### Baking Privacy In, Not Bolting It On

Article 25 of GDPR establishes the principle of **Privacy by Design and by Default** — arguably one of the most transformative requirements in modern data protection law.

**Privacy by Design** means that privacy protections must be built into systems, processes, and products from the outset — not added on as an afterthought after a product is built or deployed.

**Privacy by Default** means that, by default, only the minimum personal data necessary for each specific purpose should be processed, automatically — individuals should not have to take extra steps to protect their privacy.

### What This Means in Practice

**Product and system development:** When building a new feature, tool, or system that processes personal data, privacy considerations must be incorporated from the design phase. Conducting a **Data Protection Impact Assessment (DPIA)** may be required.

**Marketing:** Default newsletter settings should not include pre-ticked boxes for all possible communications — default should be minimum communications, with users opting in for more.

**Data collection forms:** Default settings should collect minimum data; optional fields should be clearly marked; form fields for data that isn't needed shouldn't exist.

**Employee tools:** When adopting a new SaaS tool that handles personal data, a privacy review (and potentially a DPIA) should be completed before deployment.

### Data Protection Impact Assessments (DPIAs)

A DPIA is required when a type of processing is likely to result in high risk to the rights and freedoms of individuals. DPIAs are mandatory for:

- Systematic and extensive profiling producing legal/significant effects
- Large-scale processing of special category data
- Systematic monitoring of publicly accessible areas (CCTV)
- New technologies with unclear privacy impacts

**Your role:** If you are involved in launching a new product, system, or data processing activity, flag it to your DPO or privacy team early — before development begins — so a DPIA can be assessed and conducted if needed.

## SECTION 10: International Data Transfers

### Why Cross-Border Transfers Are Regulated

One of GDPR's most distinctive features is its strict regulation of transfers of personal data to countries outside the EU/EEA. The rationale: the protections GDPR provides should not be circumvented simply by moving data to a country with weaker privacy laws.

### What Counts as a Transfer?

A "transfer" is broader than just physically moving a file:

- Storing EU data on servers located outside the EU
- Allowing an employee in the US to access EU customer records
- Using a cloud service provider whose servers are outside the EU
- Emailing EU customer data to a partner in a third country
- Granting remote access to a system containing EU data to a non-EU contractor

### Permitted Mechanisms for International Transfers

**Adequacy Decision (Article 45):** The European Commission has determined that certain countries provide an adequate level of data protection — data can transfer freely. Current adequate countries include: UK (for now), Canada (commercial organizations), Japan, South Korea, Israel, Switzerland, and others.

**Standard Contractual Clauses (SCCs) (Article 46):** EU-approved contract templates that impose GDPR-equivalent obligations on the recipient. The most widely used mechanism for transfers to non-adequate countries (including the US in many contexts).

**Binding Corporate Rules (BCRs):** Legally binding internal rules for multinational companies transferring data internally across borders.

**Specific Derogations (Article 49):** Used only in exceptional circumstances — explicit consent of the data subject, performance of a contract in the data subject's interest, vital interests, etc. These are narrow exceptions, not default mechanisms.

### The US Transfer Problem

The EU-US relationship for data transfers has been turbulent. After two previous frameworks were invalidated (Safe Harbor in 2015, Privacy Shield in 2020), the **EU-US Data Privacy Framework** was adopted in 2023, allowing transfers to certified US organizations.

However, organizations relying on this framework should stay current on its status and maintain SCCs as a backup mechanism.

**Practical guidance:** Before transferring EU personal data to any non-EU country or allowing non-EU access:

1. Confirm whether an adequacy decision covers the destination country
2. If not, ensure SCCs or BCRs are in place
3. Consult your privacy team for cloud providers and vendor contracts
4. Never transfer EU data to a third country without confirming the lawful transfer mechanism

## SECTION 11: Personal Data Breaches — Recognition and Response

### What Is a Personal Data Breach?

Under GDPR, a personal data breach is any security incident that leads to the accidental or unlawful:
- **Destruction** of personal data
- **Loss** of personal data
- **Alteration** of personal data
- **Unauthorized disclosure** of personal data
- **Unauthorized access** to personal data

Breaches encompass more than just hacking. Common examples:

| Incident | Breach? |
|---------|---------|
| Laptop with customer data stolen | Yes |
| Email with customer list sent to wrong address | Yes |
| Paper records left on a train | Yes |
| Ransomware encrypts customer database | Yes (availability breach) |
| Employee accesses records without authorization | Yes (confidentiality breach) |
| USB drive with employee data lost | Yes |
| Server misconfiguration exposes user data publicly | Yes |
| Accidental deletion of a database without backup | Yes (availability/integrity breach) |

### GDPR Breach Notification Timeline — 72 Hours

This is one of GDPR's most demanding requirements: once you become **aware** of a personal data breach, you have **72 hours** to notify your supervisory authority (e.g., the ICO in the UK, DPA in Germany, CNIL in France).

**This is 72 hours — not 72 business hours.** Weekends count.

Notification to the supervisory authority is not required if the breach is unlikely to result in a risk to the rights and freedoms of individuals (e.g., an encrypted laptop is lost with no key compromise).

**Individual notification:** When a breach is likely to result in **high risk** to individuals, they must be notified **without undue delay** — meaning as soon as possible, not after a lengthy investigation.

### Your Role in Breach Response

**If you discover or suspect a breach:**

1. **Stop the breach if possible** without destroying evidence (e.g., close a misconfigured sharing link, remove an unauthorized user's access)
2. **Report immediately** to your manager AND the DPO/privacy team — do not wait to be sure, and do not attempt to investigate or remediate without guidance
3. **Document** what happened, when you noticed it, what data may be involved, and how many individuals may be affected
4. **Preserve evidence** — do not delete logs, emails, or records that may be relevant
5. **Do not notify individuals yourself** unless instructed to by the privacy team

**The 72-hour clock starts when the organization becomes aware** — which means the clock can effectively start when YOU discover a breach and report it. Every hour of delay in internal reporting is an hour less for the privacy team to conduct their assessment and file with the regulator.

### After a Breach

All breaches must be documented internally, even if they don't meet the threshold for regulatory notification. Your organization must maintain a record of:

- Nature of the breach
- Categories and approximate number of individuals affected
- Categories and approximate number of personal data records affected
- Likely consequences
- Measures taken or proposed to address the breach

## SECTION 12: Employee Responsibilities Under GDPR

### Your Daily GDPR Obligations

GDPR is not just a legal and technical framework — it is a behavioral framework. Every employee who touches personal data has responsibilities:

**Before collecting or processing personal data:**
- Confirm there is a lawful basis for the processing
- Ensure data subjects have been informed (through a privacy notice)
- Collect only the data you need (data minimisation)
- Confirm that appropriate security measures are in place

**When handling personal data:**
- Process it only for the stated, documented purpose
- Keep it secure — appropriate access controls, encryption, secure storage
- Do not share it with unauthorized parties
- Do not transfer it internationally without a confirmed lawful mechanism
- Do not retain it longer than the organization's retention schedule specifies

**When responding to individuals:**
- Route data subject requests (access, erasure, correction) immediately to the privacy team
- Do not fulfill these requests yourself unless you are specifically authorized and trained to do so
- Do not deny or dismiss requests — even if you're unsure whether they're valid, route them up

**When something goes wrong:**
- Report data breaches immediately — every minute matters
- Do not attempt to cover up incidents or fix them quietly
- Cooperate fully with breach investigations

### The DPO — Your Privacy Partner

Organizations that meet certain criteria under GDPR must appoint a **Data Protection Officer (DPO)**. The DPO is an expert in data protection law who:

- Advises on GDPR compliance
- Monitors compliance programs
- Acts as the contact point for supervisory authorities
- Provides guidance on DPIAs

**The DPO is independent and must not be instructed by management on how to perform their data protection tasks.** The DPO reports directly to the highest level of management.

If your organization has a DPO, you should know who they are and how to contact them. If you have a data protection question that your manager cannot answer, the DPO is your resource.

### Records of Processing Activities (RoPA)

GDPR requires organizations to maintain written records of all processing activities (Article 30). This is an internal document that describes:

- The purposes of processing
- Categories of personal data and data subjects
- Retention periods
- Recipients and international transfers
- Security measures

As an employee, you may be asked to provide information for the RoPA when your team takes on new data processing activities. Take these requests seriously — the RoPA is a key compliance document audited by regulators.

## SECTION 13: Summary and Key Takeaways

You have completed the content for Module 3. Here are the most critical points to remember:

1. **GDPR applies based on where individuals are located** — not where your company is based
2. **Personal data is broad** — IP addresses, cookie IDs, and voice recordings are personal data
3. **Special category data needs extra protection** — health, race, religion, biometrics, etc.
4. **Every processing activity needs a lawful basis** — determine it before you start
5. **Consent must be freely given, specific, informed, and documented** — it's not the easy option
6. **Collect only what you need** — data minimisation is a fundamental principle
7. **Data has an expiry date** — follow the retention schedule; delete when no longer needed
8. **Data subjects have eight rights** — route all requests to the privacy team immediately
9. **Privacy by Design** — think about privacy before starting a project, not after
10. **Breach reporting is 72 hours** — report suspected breaches immediately; the clock is always running
